That classification depends on treating notebook buyers as the existing market and planners as a new product. Selling through a new website alone would not necessarily change the market. State the baseline before assigning a growth label.

03

Calculate the portfolio effect

Cannibalisation is 400 displaced Classic units divided by 1,000 planner units: 40%. Planner contribution is 1,000 × EGP 30 = EGP 30,000. Lost Classic contribution is 400 × EGP 25 = EGP 10,000. Subtracting EGP 15,000 launch spending leaves a forecast improvement of EGP 5,000 versus not launching, with other fixed costs unchanged.

04

Choose with conditions

A limited launch is defensible because the stated forecast produces a positive incremental result. Before approval, test whether 600 planner purchases would truly add sales and whether production can supply them without displacing other profitable orders. Review cash timing and repeat demand as well. A BCG label alone is not a reason to fund or abandon anything.

Check your reasoning
  • Did I divide by the largest rival’s share?
  • Did I subtract contribution lost on displaced sales?
  • Did I distinguish the forecast from proof of demand?

BrightFold: when higher sales earn less

One defensible line of reasoning, not an official model answer.

Evidence→Choice→Action→Measure
01

Separate revenue from contribution

Without promotion, revenue is 1,000 × EGP 200 = EGP 200,000; unit contribution is EGP 80, so total contribution is EGP 80,000. With promotion, revenue is 1,400 × EGP 180 = EGP 252,000. Unit contribution falls to EGP 60, giving EGP 84,000 before the additional campaign spending.

02

Compare like with like

The promotional result after campaign spending is EGP 84,000 − EGP 10,000 = EGP 74,000. Compared with EGP 80,000 without promotion, the change is negative EGP 6,000. Revenue rises by EGP 52,000, but the reduced contribution per bag and added spending outweigh the forecast volume gain. Neither result is total company net profit.

03

Solve the volume requirement

Let Q be promotional bags sold in the month. Matching the baseline requires Q × EGP 60 − EGP 10,000 = EGP 80,000. Therefore Q = 1,500 bags: 500 more than the 1,000-bag baseline, a 50% volume increase. This is the volume needed to match the baseline contribution after campaign spending, not the company’s overall break-even volume.

04

Test a revised choice

One reasonable recommendation is to reject the proposal at its current forecast and test a smaller, explicitly costed alternative. Compare similar randomly assigned customer groups where practical, tracking incremental contribution and later purchases. Check whether discounted orders replace full-price orders or pull demand forward. A smaller discount or better targeting needs its own calculation and evidence.

BeamNest: make the message match the channel

One defensible line of reasoning, not an official model answer.

Evidence→Choice→Action→Measure
01

Match coverage to service capability

A staged, selective rollout through two ready shops is a plausible choice. Shops demonstrate components, hold agreed stock and hand over orders; BeamNest maintains product guidance, accurate availability and the ordering system. Agree who handles returns and support. Existing agreements need consideration before changing access, and direct online sales still require fulfilment and service capacity.

02

Give each communication a job

Push activity could train shop staff using a common demonstration and component checklist. Pull activity could invite local customers to book a demonstration at a ready shop. Both should promise help choosing a suitable combination and state confirmed collection timing. The “collect today” claim needs revision wherever live availability and fulfilment cannot support it.

03

Make readiness observable

The channel manager could own launch readiness, with shop managers and operations responsible for their tasks. Before local advertising begins, verify staff competence, component availability, order confirmation and the collection handover through a test order. Broader advertising should follow the remaining shops’ readiness. Record failures and fix their causes before declaring the customer promise deliverable.

04

ThreadBox: turn customer scores into action

One defensible line of reasoning, not an official model answer.

Evidence→Choice→Action→Measure
01

Use all valid NPS responses

Promoters are 36 ÷ 80 = 45% and detractors are 20 ÷ 80 = 25%, so NPS is +20. The 24 passives remain in the 80-response denominator. Response rate is 80 ÷ 200 = 40%. Report NPS as a score, and recognise that respondents may differ from the customers who did not answer.

02

Keep experiences and behaviour distinct

Using the stated top-two-box method, delivery CSAT is 42 ÷ 60 = 70%. Its survey response rate is 60 ÷ 100 = 60%. The April cohort’s repeat-purchase rate is 120 ÷ 300 = 40% within 90 days. Survey respondents and purchasing customers are different bases; these percentages answer different questions and cannot be substituted for one another.

03

Diagnose before choosing an incentive

A discount may not fix missing instructions. Eighteen of 60 delivery respondents, or 30%, mentioned that problem; this is not a proven defect rate across all deliveries. Inspect packing records and contact consenting customers to understand the failures. The data do not establish that detractors received incomplete kits or that missing instructions caused the observed repeat rate.

04

Close the control loop

One possible action is a pack-out checklist owned by the fulfilment lead, with a proposed target of at least 98% instruction inclusion across all inspected kits over four weeks. Record the baseline first, provide missing instructions to affected customers, and repeat the same delivery survey method. Review completeness, CSAT and contribution before considering a separate incentive test.
